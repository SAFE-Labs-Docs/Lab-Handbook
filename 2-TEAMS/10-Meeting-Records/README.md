# I commit to establish a mechanism to record key outcomes from each 1-on-1 meeting

This page collects real-world examples of meeting record-keeping approaches from labs around the world. We encourage all labs implementing the SAFE Labs Handbook to share their own approach here. To contribute, open a pull request that adds your example to this README under `## Examples`, below the `###` heading for your country (add the heading if it is missing, naming the country as the map does, such as United States or United Kingdom). Write the example as `>_[LabName_YEAR](URL)_: text`, quoting your text exactly and starting every further line of it with `>`, blank lines included, and leave a blank line after the country heading and between examples. The link is optional, but only linked examples appear on the safelabs.info map.

## Details
Commonly reported frustrations of the 1-on-1 meeting process in labs are:

1. Lab members feel that their group leader forgets the content of their previous meeting and so appears disengaged, or time is wasted rehashing the previous meeting.
2. Group leaders feel that a previously agreed course of action has not been followed (or even attempted) and the lab member has instead pursued an unrelated tangent.

Recording the key outcomes of each 1-on-1 meeting immediately after it takes place has been reported to robustly improve these issues. It allows any miscommunication to be readily identified, and provides a way for all participants to refresh their memory before the next meeting, saving time and confusion. This does not mean lab members should never diverge from the proposed plan, but if they do, they should be ready to justify that decision in future meetings.

## Suggestions
- Require lab members to summarise each meeting in a shared document.
- Include agreed-upon action points to prioritise before the next meeting.
- Lab members should take notes during the meeting.
- Group leader should read the document after/before meetings (to catch misunderstandings).
- Other solutions include allowing the lab member to record meetings (e.g. via zoom).

## Examples

### Canada
>_Anonymous lab_:
>
>Each lab member will have a designated Google Doc (visible only to you and [the PI]) for agendas and notes for our weekly meetings. (Following templates posted on Twitter by Susana Wadgymar and Michael Hoffman.) By 11am on the day of our weekly meeting, update this file with the following information (don’t spend any more than 15 minutes on this, and don’t feel the need to use complete sentences):
>- Make a short agenda of any things you’d like to discuss in our meeting, in addition to your research plans and progress (if you have anything else to discuss, which may not be the case). I may add to these as well.
>- Annotate the plans from the previous week using the ‘Comment’ feature or by adding text in a different color. We’ll use these notes as a tool for assessing whether we are developing SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-Bound) plans
>- Write a brief self-reflection on the previous week. This reflection is open-ended, but I suggest discussing what went well, what you found to be the most challenging, what you did to try to overcome those challenges, lessons learned, things that I/others can help with, and notes to yourself on what you would do differently in the upcoming weeks.
>- Draft a list of plans and priorities for the upcoming week.

>Monthly meetings:  Monthly small-group meetings serve a complementary purpose to weekly progress meetings: each trainee summarizes recent progress, sets goals for the coming month, and hears from peers about adjacent projects, fostering broader scientific awareness across the lab.

One new set of results or experiments can be presented in details but other new results should be presented during the bi-weekly progress meetings or during unscheduled check-ins (see below). >At least once a year, one monthly meeting should be dedicated entirely to publication planning — ideally following a research lab meeting presentation. For that session, trainees are expected to prepare a story outline indicating which results will be presented in each figure.
